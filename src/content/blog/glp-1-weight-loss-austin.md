Shopping for GLP-1 care in Austin can feel like comparing identical offers with completely different prices. They are often not identical. The product, indication, prescriber relationship, monitoring, insurance path, and legal basis for any compounding can all differ.

The safest comparison begins before the monthly price: identify exactly what drug is being discussed and how the practice decides whether it is appropriate.

## GLP-1 is a drug class, not one product

Semaglutide is the active ingredient in several FDA-approved products with different labeled indications, including Wegovy and Ozempic. Tirzepatide is the active ingredient in FDA-approved Zepbound and Mounjaro. A brand name and indication matter; one product's approval should not be borrowed to market another product or a compounded preparation.

For weight management, FDA-approved Wegovy and Zepbound have specific indications, contraindications, warnings, dose-escalation instructions, and patient labeling. Read the current FDA prescribing information for [Wegovy](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/215256s024lbl.pdf) and [Zepbound](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/217806s042lbl.pdf), then discuss how it applies to you with a licensed clinician.

## Approved and compounded products are different

Compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide is not an FDA-approved generic version of a brand drug. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are dispensed.

The national shortages of semaglutide injection and tirzepatide injection were declared resolved, and the shortage-based enforcement-discretion periods ended in 2025. FDA reiterated in April 2026 that 503A compounding must be based on a prescription for an individual patient and is restricted from regularly making products that are essentially copies of commercially available drugs, unless the prescriber documents a change that produces a significant difference for an identified patient. Read [FDA's current GLP-1 compounding update](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-clarifies-policies-compounders-national-glp-1-supply-begins-stabilize).

That means a website cannot responsibly promise a compounded GLP-1 to everyone who chooses a tier. Availability and appropriateness depend on current law, the pharmacy, the commercially available options, and a documented individual evaluation.

FDA has also published concerns about dosing errors, fraudulent labels, improper storage, salt forms of semaglutide, and adverse-event reports involving unapproved GLP-1 products. Its [consumer safety page](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fdas-concerns-unapproved-glp-1-drugs-used-weight-loss) is worth reading before buying from any online source.

## What a real evaluation should clarify

A website cannot determine candidacy. A clinician may need to review:

- The goal and whether it fits an approved indication or another lawful clinical rationale
- Medical history, current medication, allergies, and prior treatment
- Pregnancy plans and other contraindications or precautions
- Symptoms or conditions that may require in-person or specialty evaluation
- The exact product, pharmacy, instructions, monitoring, and total cost
- How urgent symptoms and non-urgent side effects should be handled

The exact evaluation and monitoring are individualized. A universal lab list or internet dose ladder should not replace the product label and clinician judgment.

## Common risks deserve more than a footnote

The FDA-approved labels describe common gastrointestinal adverse reactions and important warnings and precautions. These include risks involving pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, dehydration-related kidney injury, severe gastrointestinal reactions, and hypoglycemia in certain medication combinations. Both labels contain boxed warnings concerning thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents and contraindications for people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2.

This is not a complete safety list. Patients should receive the current medication guide and product-specific instructions. Severe or persistent symptoms require prompt clinical direction; emergencies require 911 or emergency care.

## Compare the whole care model

Ask each Austin or telehealth provider the same questions:

1. What exact product are you discussing: FDA-approved brand or compounded preparation?
2. If compounded, what documented patient-specific need supports it, and which pharmacy dispenses it?
3. Who performs the evaluation, and will the same clinician handle follow-up?
4. What is included in the fee: visit, medication, supplies, delivery, lab work, and prior authorization?
5. What happens if the product is unavailable, not tolerated, not appropriate, or not covered?
6. Where do clinical messages go, and how are urgent concerns handled?
7. What minimum term and cancellation rules apply?

Be cautious when a site calls a compounded drug “the same as,” “generic,” or “clinically proven equivalent to” an FDA-approved product; promises a prescription before evaluation; or hides the pharmacy and full cost.
