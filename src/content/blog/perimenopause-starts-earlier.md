Menopause is identified after twelve consecutive months without a menstrual period when another cause does not explain it. Perimenopause, also called the menopausal transition, is the time leading up to that point. Symptoms and cycle changes can begin years before the final period.

That does not mean every sleep, mood, weight, or concentration change in midlife is caused by hormones. It means the timing and full pattern deserve a careful history rather than a one-number conclusion.

## When can the transition begin?

The National Institute on Aging says most women begin the menopausal transition between ages 45 and 55, though timing varies and the process may last several years. Some people experience changes earlier, and an unusually early loss of periods may need a different evaluation. Read the NIH overview of [what menopause is and how the transition is assessed](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/menopause/what-menopause).

Age alone does not confirm the cause of a symptom. Current contraception, pregnancy possibility, thyroid conditions, medication effects, sleep disorders, anemia, mental-health conditions, and other factors can overlap with common menopause concerns.

## Symptoms can involve more than hot flashes

Commonly reported changes include:

- Irregular, shorter, longer, lighter, or heavier periods
- Hot flashes and night sweats
- Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or feeling rested
- Vaginal dryness, pain with sex, or urinary concerns
- Mood, memory, or concentration changes
- Joint or muscle discomfort

The pattern varies widely. Some people have few symptoms; others have symptoms that materially affect daily life. The NIH and [MedlinePlus menopause overview](https://medlineplus.gov/menopause.html) provide plain-language summaries.

## Bleeding changes should not be dismissed automatically

Cycle changes can be part of perimenopause, but not every change should be attributed to it. NIH advises clinical evaluation for concerns such as very heavy bleeding, periods occurring very close together, bleeding between periods or after sex, periods lasting more than a week, or bleeding after a full year without a period.

Seek urgent care for severe bleeding, fainting, chest pain, shortness of breath, or another emergency symptom. A telehealth marketing page is not an emergency service.

## One hormone result may not answer the question

Menopause evaluation often begins with age, menstrual pattern, symptoms, pregnancy possibility, and medical history. Hormone levels fluctuate during the transition, so a single result does not always confirm or exclude perimenopause. In some situations, including unexpectedly early changes or symptoms that could have another cause, testing can be useful.

The point is not “labs never matter” or “labs always come first.” Testing should answer a specific question or support safe treatment and monitoring.

## Treatment is an individualized benefit-risk decision

Options may include lifestyle changes, nonhormonal treatments, and menopausal hormone therapy. Hormone therapy is FDA-approved for certain menopause-related indications and is considered an effective treatment for bothersome vasomotor symptoms. It is not right for everyone, and the decision depends on symptoms, age and timing, medical and family history, route, dose, preventive-care needs, and personal preferences.

Read the FDA's current [menopause and hormone-therapy overview](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/womens-health-topics/menopause) and The Menopause Society's [patient guide to hormone therapy](https://menopause.org/patient-education/menopause-topics/hormone-therapy).

FDA-approved and compounded hormone products are not interchangeable labels. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are dispensed. FDA says it does not have evidence that compounded “bioidentical” hormones are safer or more effective than FDA-approved menopause hormone therapy. A compounded preparation should be considered only for an identified patient-specific need under applicable law, not as a universal upgrade.

## Questions to bring to a visit

You do not need to arrive with a self-diagnosis. A short written timeline can make the conversation more useful:

- What changed, when it began, and how often it happens
- Menstrual or bleeding pattern, if applicable
- Sleep, mood, sexual-health, urinary, temperature, and pain concerns
- Current medications, supplements, and contraception
- Personal and family medical history
- What you most want to improve and what risks concern you

Ask why a test is or is not being ordered, whether an option is FDA-approved or compounded, what alternatives exist, and what follow-up would change the plan.
